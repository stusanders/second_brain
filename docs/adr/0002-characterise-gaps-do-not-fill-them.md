# The system characterises gaps; it does not fill them

Where a document jumps from evidence to conclusion without stating the connecting step, the
system records that a step is missing, records the surrounding context, and stops. It does
not propose the missing premise, even though it easily could and even though doing so would
make the output look far more complete.

## Why

A model asked to supply a missing premise always succeeds, fluently, whether or not any
reasoning ever existed there. Some decisions were political, or contingent, or a minister
said no — and no plausible reconstruction of those is anything but fiction. Manufacturing a
justification that never existed, in convincing departmental prose, is worse in a government
setting than having no system at all: it is wrong in exactly the register that gets believed.

There is a second, more practical reason. Argument structure is not fully determined by the
text, and expert readers disagree about it. If the system outputs a proposition, expert
disagreement is fatal to it. If it outputs a question, disagreement is survivable — the
expert arbitrates, and their answer becomes the artifact.

## Consequences

The gaps become the product rather than a defect to be engineered away: the first
user-facing output is a targeted question list for the departing expert, not a completed
map. That is a harder thing to demo and the only route to knowledge the documents
structurally cannot hold.

It also means the artifact will look incomplete, permanently and by design. Anyone
reviewing it needs to be told that up front, or they will read the holes as a bug.
